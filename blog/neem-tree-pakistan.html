Neem: Pakistan's Village Pharmacy — and How to Plant It Right

20 October 2026 By Ahmad Baloch, Founder ShajarDost 7 min read

Ask your grandfather which tree he would plant if he could plant only one, and in most of Punjab the answer will be neem. Azadirachta indica — native to South Asia and long naturalized across Pakistan, India, Bangladesh and beyond — is the tree that gives shade in the worst heat, leaves that repel insects, wood that resists termites, and a crown that stays green deep into the dry season. It earned the nickname "the village pharmacy" honestly. But neem also has a hard limit almost every viral post skips: it cannot take frost, and it refuses to grow in the wrong soil. This guide covers both sides — what research says neem can do, and where it honestly fails — so your neem becomes a 100-year companion instead of a one-winter casualty.

1. Why Neem Earned Its Reputation

Winrock International's authoritative fact sheet on neem reads like a survivalist's résumé. Once established, neem is highly drought tolerant and can survive 7–8 month dry seasons; with groundwater within reach, it can get by on rainfall as low as 150 mm a year, though it performs best in zones receiving 450–1200 mm — which covers most of agricultural Punjab. Its wood is hard, durable, aromatic and resistant to insects and fungi, which is why neem timbers last in village construction. And its leaves and seed kernels carry azadirachtin, the compound that repels pests, stops insects from feeding, and disrupts their growth and reproduction — the basis of commercially produced neem pesticides in several countries. No wonder Pakistani farmers have planted it beside wells and boundary walls for generations.

Growth is rapid for roughly the first five years after the roots establish, then slows gradually as the tree settles into its long middle age — a mature neem reaches 15–25 metres and spreads a crown broad enough to shade a small courtyard or a farm-tractor with trailer. Per a National Institutes of Health book chapter on neem, a tree normally begins bearing fruit after 3–5 years, becomes fully productive at about 10 years, and can then produce up to 50 kg of fruit annually — fruit whose dried kernels carry 40–50% oil. That is a serious annual yield from a tree you plant once.

2. Where Neem Grows — and Where It Refuses To

Neem's tolerance is wide but not total, and the "not" list is where most failures happen. The Winrock fact sheet is precise. On the positive side: neem thrives on rocky, dry, shallow and infertile soils, and grows across a soil pH range of roughly 5.0–8.0 (it prefers near-neutral 6.2–7.0). On the negative side, neem is not recommended for saline soils — a real issue in parts of southern Punjab — nor for silty or micaceous loams, silty clays, or sites with a subsurface hardpan. And one absolute rule: never plant neem on waterlogged or seasonally inundated land. A neem's roots need to breathe; standing water is a death sentence, which is why the tree loves well-drained rises and hates canal-seepage flats.

How do you test your spot before you commit? Simple field checks: dig the intended pit a week early and see whether it holds water after rain (if it does, pick another spot or build a raised mound); taste-test the soil — a salty crust or a white efflorescence in the dry season is a warning for saline ground; and look at the neighbourhood's old neems. In our Kasur experience, the surest sign a site suits neem is a 30-year-old neem already thriving within a kilometre. If no neem has ever survived nearby, ask why before planting a row of them.

3. Planting Right: Season, Spacing, First-Year Water

Timing in Punjab follows the rule we explain in our care guide: plant at the monsoon onset, July–August, when soil moisture at depth and easing temperatures give a new sapling its best start — the Punjab Forest Department's own guidance treats this as the natural plantation window. Dig a pit about 2×2×2 feet, mix the excavated soil with well-rotted farmyard manure, and set the sapling so the nursery soil line sits level with the ground. Water deeply immediately after planting, then follow the deep-and-rare schedule for new trees — daily for the first couple of weeks, roughly weekly through the growing season. A thick mulch ring kept away from the trunk will do the rest of the early work; our first-year care guide has that whole method, sourced.

Spacing depends on your purpose, and neem's fact sheet gives real numbers: fuelwood or timber plantations run 2.5 m × 2.5 m, thinned later to 5 m × 5 m; windbreaks use about 4 m × 2 m; and if you want maximum fruit, give each tree wide spacing so the crown can develop fully — a crowded neem grows tall and thin and fruits modestly. For a farmhouse or school ground, treat one neem as a specimen: 8–10 metres from buildings, away from septic lines, and where its eventual 15-metre crown has room to spread over shade-seekers, not over walls.

4. The Frost Warning Nobody Gives You

Here is the paragraph that saves your sapling. Neem cannot withstand cold or frost — its normal temperature range runs roughly 9.5°C to 37°C. In southern Punjab and Sindh's lowlands, mature neems shrug off ordinary winters. But northern and central Punjab plains do get frosty nights in December–January, and a young neem in its first two winters is genuinely vulnerable: leaves scorch black, young stems can die back to the ground. We have lost nursery-stage neems to exactly one bad cold snap.

The protection is cheap and worth planning at planting time. Wrap the young stem with jute sacking (bori) through December–January for the first two winters; heap a thick mulch ring to insulate the root zone; keep the tree's south-western approach open to winter sun; and resist heavy watering in cold weeks, because wet soil plus frost is worse than dry soil plus frost. By its third winter a neem's bark has thickened enough to handle ordinary Punjab cold — after that, the tree that once needed your jute sack will outlive everyone who planted it.

5. Neem From Seed: Only If You Sow Fresh

Neem seeds come with a trap: they do not store. Research from Wageningen University on neem seed storage found that at ordinary room conditions — 20°C and 75% relative humidity — neem seeds almost completely lost their ability to germinate within six months, and field practice is blunter: sow within days or weeks of picking. So if you want a neem from seed, collect ripe fruit in season (yellowish pulp, usually early summer from fruiting trees), remove the pulp, and sow the fresh kernel the same week in a nursery bag or directly in the prepared pit. Store nothing; share surplus with a neighbour instead — a neighbour with a neem is a friend with shade.

The patience ledger, from the NIH chapter: first fruit at 3–5 years, full production at about 10, then up to 50 kg of fruit a year. If that timeline sounds long, remember what you are buying: a century-scale shade and pest-repellent system for the price of one sapling. And for families who want the faster emotional win, our mango-from-seed project sprouts in weeks — neem is the same joy at a slower, grander pace.

6. What Neem Is Not

  • Not a courtyard shrub. A mature neem is a 15–25 metre tree. Plant it for the next generation's shade, at a distance from buildings — not two feet from your boundary wall.
  • Not for salty or wet land. Saline soil, silty clay, hardpan, or any spot that holds standing water will defeat it. Choose another species from our best trees for Punjab list instead.
  • Not a frost-proof tree. Protect it for its first two winters in frost-prone districts, then it stands on its own.
  • Not medicine, as far as this article is concerned. Azadirachtin's pest-control action is genuine science and commercial neem pesticides are real products — but home-remedy and health claims about neem are outside what we test or advise. We plant trees; for anything health-related, ask a qualified professional.
  • Not instant shade. The first five years are the fast ones, but a young neem still needs the full watering and mulch routine of any new tree.

The honesty part: buy the sapling, then buy the care

Neem's toughness is real but it is earned after establishment. A sapling's first summer and first winter are exactly as demanding as any other young tree — skip the deep watering, the mulch ring and the frost wrap, and no amount of natural drought-tolerance will save it. Our own care ledger for 500 mixed trees, at Rs.802 per tree per year, applies to neem like everything else we plant. The good news: pay those three years, and neem repays you for the next fifty to one hundred.
