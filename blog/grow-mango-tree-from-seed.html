Grow a Mango Tree From the Mango You Already Ate

19 October 2026 By Ahmad Baloch, Founder ShajarDost 6 min read

Here is a secret the nurseries don't mind telling you, because it still won't put them out of business: every mango you eat contains a ready-made tree. No shopping, no delivery, no budget. The leftover gutthi (seed) on your plate, right now, is a living plant with its alarm clock set — it is only waiting for soil, water and warmth. This is the cheapest, most addictive tree project in Pakistan, it works for kids and grandparents alike, and it has made more lifelong tree-lovers than any campaign poster ever will. Here is exactly how to do it, plus one honest paragraph about fruit that most guides skip.

What you need

  • One fresh mango — eat it. (Fresh matters: a seed that has dried out for weeks wakes up grumpy or not at all.)
  • A pot or any container with drainage holes, filled with loose soil or compost.
  • A knife or sturdy scissors (grown-ups only for this part) and a warm, bright spot — a balcony, courtyard or windowsill.

The 8 steps

  1. Eat the mango, save the seed. The big white husk inside is the seed's armour. Scrape off any pulp clinging to it — pulp invites mould.
  2. Dry the husk for a day or two in shade. Not on a hot roof — you want it dry on the surface, not cooked.
  3. Carefully open the husk. Snip or pry along the edge with scissors or a knife, like opening a small clam, and lift out the inner seed — a pale bean the size of a big date. This step is optional (some people plant the whole husk), but opening it can speed sprouting by a week or two and lets you see what you've got.
  4. Plant it 2–3 cm deep, flat or slightly on its edge, in moist (not soaked) soil. Mangoes sprout best in warmth — summer rooms are perfect; avoid cold store-rooms in winter.
  5. Water lightly every couple of days. The soil should feel like a squeezed sponge — damp, never swampy. Overwatering is the #1 killer of waiting seeds; rot claims them quietly underground.
  6. Wait 1–4 weeks. Then the fun surprise: one seed can send up two or three sprouts at once (mangoes can carry multiple embryos in one seed). If that happens, let them grow a little and gently separate them into their own pots — congratulations, you got free trees on top of your free tree.
  7. Give it sun once it's up. A mango seedling wants bright light. In a month or two it will be knee-high; when it reaches about 30–60 cm and has a few woody stems, move it to a bigger pot or into the ground.
  8. Plant it out in the right season — the monsoon window or cool season, never into a punishing May. Choose a spot with full sun and room: a mango grows into a big, generous tree, so keep it well away from walls and water lines (see our species guide for placement sense).

The three-year promise (you knew we'd say it)

We run plantations around Pattoki, and here is our rule from the field: the day you plant is day one of three years of care. For a young mango that means deep watering whenever the top two inches of soil dry out, a thick mulch of dry leaves around the base to hold moisture through April–June, and protection from cows, goats and cricket bats — in our published ledger, protection (a watchman) costs more than water, because animals and people kill more young trees than drought does. The full arithmetic of those three years is in our real-cost article; the short version is that care, not planting, is what turns a stick into a tree. A school can turn this into a class project: name the tree, measure it monthly, and let the children keep the record.

The honest paragraph about fruit

Will your seed-grown mango give fruit? Eventually, maybe — but read this before you promise anyone aam ka season. Most famous Pakistani varieties (Chaunsa, Sindhri, Anwar Ratol) are reproduced by grafting, precisely because a seed does not reliably copy its parent: fruiting can take 5–8+ years, and the fruit may turn out smaller, different or nothing like the mango you ate. (A handful of "polyembryonic" varieties do come true from seed and can even fruit in 2–4 years — one reason a mystery seed is a lottery ticket worth holding.) So grow the seed for shade, fun and learning; buy a grafted sapling when you want guaranteed mangoes. Our Free Plants wall exists exactly for that.

Why this silly little project matters

A child who watches a seed open its back and stand up does not forget it. That is the entire reason our Green Scouts programme starts with seeds and pots before it ever touches a plantation. One mango tree, full grown, shades a street corner for fifty years — and the person who planted it, if they are lucky, gets to say "I grew that from my lunch" for all fifty. Start with one. The second one happens on its own; they always do.
