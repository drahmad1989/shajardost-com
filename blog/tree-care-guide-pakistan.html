You Planted a Tree. Now What?

19 October 2026 By Ahmad Baloch, Founder ShajarDost 8 min read

Planting a sapling takes five minutes. Keeping it alive for the next three years is the part almost everybody underestimates — and it is exactly where the difference between a green Pakistan and a photo-op lives. In our first myth-buster we showed you the UK government trial where plots with proper aftercare kept 79% of their trees alive versus 47% without it; same saplings, same soil, same rain — the only difference was care. This guide is the care. It is written for Pakistan's climate and our real field costs, and every recommendation in it comes from a named source: the Arbor Day Foundation's watering guidance, Kansas State University's extension research on mulch, and the Punjab Forest Department's monsoon planting advice — plus three years of our own ledger from Pattoki.

1. Water: Deep and Rare Beats Sprinkles Every Day

The most common way kind-hearted people kill trees is not neglect — it is love, applied as a daily sprinkle. Two minutes of light watering wets only the top inch of soil, so the young tree grows lazy, shallow roots that die in the first real dry spell. The Arbor Day Foundation's guidance is precise: water newly planted trees once a day for the first couple of weeks, then about once a week through the growing season — and make each watering deep, meaning the soil is moist all the way down to the depth of the roots. Deep, infrequent water pushes roots downward, and deep roots are what carry a tree through its second summer on its own.

How do you know when to water? Use the trowel test the Arborists use: push a small blade about two inches into the soil, open a little trench, and touch the soil with a finger. Moist means wait; dry means water. And remember their warning — over-watering is one of the most common tree-care mistakes. Soil that is always soggy has no oxygen in it, and roots suffocate just as surely as they dry. In Punjab's summer heat, water early morning or evening so it soaks in instead of steaming off, and in peak May–June heat a young tree may genuinely need two to three deep soaks a week. Our own ledger agrees that water is serious business: for our 500-tree plot, irrigation cost Rs.96,000 in one year — second only to the watchman.

2. Mulch: The Ring That Can (Almost) Double Growth

If there is one piece of magic in this whole guide, it costs nothing. Kansas State University's extension horticulturists put it plainly: a mulch ring kept 3 to 6 feet around a young tree can almost double its growth rate. The reasons are unglamorous — mulch keeps soil moisture from evaporating, cools the root zone in June, and, just as importantly, kills the grass. Grass is not a carpet for your tree; it is a rival that robs water and nutrients and measurably delays establishment. Every tree on our plots that we circled with mulch visibly outgrew the ones we didn't.

But mulch has one sacred rule: donuts, not volcanoes. The ring starts about a hand-width (3–6 inches) away from the trunk and spreads outward, 2–4 inches thick. Piling mulch against the bark — the "volcano" you see in public parks — traps moisture against the trunk, rots the living cambium underneath, and can kill the tree outright. In Pakistan you do not need to buy mulch: last season's katai hui ghaas, dry fallen leaves, and the wood chips after any municipal pruning all work beautifully. And here is the kicker from the same K-State review: for a young tree, getting the mulch right matters more than fertilizing. Skip the fertilizer shopping; make the ring instead.

3. Protection: The Biggest Threat Has Four Legs (or a Blade)

Here is a number that surprises everyone: in our 500-tree care ledger, the single biggest expense is not water, not fertilizer, not even the plants — it is the watchman, Rs.240,000 a year. That is what three years of field work taught us protection is worth. A young sapling is delicious to every goat in the mohalla, tempting shade to every parked tractor, and invisible to the man with a grass trimmer. Kansas State's mulch guide lists a quiet killer most people never think about: mowers and weed whips knocking chunks of bark off tender trunks — a wound that never heals properly and invites rot.

Protection is cheap and unglamorous. A tree guard, a thorn (kikar) fence on rural plots, or even a painted wooden stake placed loosely beside the stem does most of the job. One caution: any rope, wire or straw tied to support the tree must be checked every few months and removed after the first year — we have rescued more than one tree being slowly strangled by the same dori that was meant to help it. Guard it, then let it stand on its own.

4. Timing: Monsoon Is Planting Season — Here's Why

Every year we watch enthusiastic volunteers plant in the wrong month and then fight an unwinnable watering battle. The Punjab Forest, Wildlife & Fisheries Department's own planting guidance explains why July and August are the natural plantation window: the soil holds moisture at depth, temperatures ease, and — the beautiful part — the monsoon does most of your watering for you. A sapling that goes into the ground at the rains' onset spends its critical first weeks in naturally moist soil instead of a watering can's schedule.

Resist the temptation to plant in June, however green the mood. That is Punjab's cruelest month for new saplings — heatwaves, bone-dry soil and unreliable pre-monsoon showers stack the odds against a plant whose roots have not yet gripped the earth. The monsoon onset is not a deadline you miss; it is a season you join. If a drive must happen outside July–August, it needs a written watering plan for the dry weeks that follow — that is exactly how our own contract plantations are structured.

6. The Kill List: Six Ways Good People Kill Good Trees

  1. Daily sprinkles. Two minutes of light water grows shallow roots that surrender at the first dry spell. Deep and rare wins.
  2. Volcano mulch. A mountain of mulch against the trunk rots the bark and kills slowly, invisibly. Donut, not volcano.
  3. Grass to the trunk. Lawn right up to the stem is a rival, not a rug — it steals the water you just paid for.
  4. The forgotten dori. Support ties left tight for years cut into the bark like wire into fruit. Check quarterly, remove after year one.
  5. June planting. Planting into pre-monsoon heat hands your sapling a drought it cannot survive. Wait for the rains.
  6. Fertilizer instead of attention. Chemicals cannot fix a grazed stem, a broken guard or bone-dry soil. Protection and mulch first — K-State's research says mulch beats fertilizer for young trees outright.

The honesty part: some trees die anyway

Even with this entire guide followed to the letter, a 70–80% survival rate is normal field reality — and it is exactly what we budget for. Our ledger carries a standing Rs.10,000 a year replacement line for 500 trees, because the honest number includes the ones we lose to heat, grazing or plain bad luck. A dead sapling that gets replaced and learned from beats an unwatered "success" every time. What is not normal is planting a thousand and checking none. Count your losses the way you count your wins.

"Ped lagana paanch minute ka kaam hai — ped zinda rakhna teen saal ka. Farq yahi dono ke beech hai." — Planting is a five-minute job; keeping the tree alive is a three-year one. That difference is everything.

Sources & data note

• Watering schedule (daily for first two weeks, then ~weekly; deep watering to root depth; over-watering as a common mistake; winter dry-spell watering): Arbor Day Foundation tree watering guidance.

• Mulch ring 3–6 ft almost doubling growth rate, grass competition, mower/trimmer bark damage, "donut not volcano" technique and mulch outweighing fertilizer for young trees: Kansas State University Research & Extension (Johnson County) — How to Mulch Trees; NC Urban Forest Council — Mulch the Right Way (2–4 inch layer, kept off the trunk).

• Monsoon July–August as Punjab's natural plantation window and soil-moisture reasoning: Punjab Forest, Wildlife & Fisheries Department public planting guidance; pre-monsoon (June) heatwave/dry-soil caution: regional plantation guidance.

• Care costs (Rs.802/tree/yr; watchman Rs.240k, water Rs.96k, replacement Rs.10k per 500 trees/yr) and 70–80% survival with replanting: ShajarDost internal care ledger, Pattoki / Kasur district — published in our cost article.
