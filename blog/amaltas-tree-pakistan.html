Amaltas: Punjab's Golden Shower — and How to Plant It for Decades of Blooms

20 October 2026 By Ahmad Baloch, Founder ShajarDost 8 min read

For a few weeks every year, when the plains heat up and every other tree looks tired, the amaltas sets itself on fire with gold. Cassia fistula — golden shower, amaltaas, the Indian laburnum — drops its leaves and then hangs whole cascades of yellow blossoms from bare branches, and every passer-by stops to photograph it. It is the tree Pakistan's cities are photographed with in early summer, the national tree of Thailand, and the state flower of Kerala. But most amaltas guides skip three things that decide whether yours ever blooms: its sun and drainage demands, the years of patience before the first flowers, and the fact that those beautiful hanging pods carry poisonous seeds. This guide covers the bloom and the fine print, honestly.

1. The Tree That Blooms With Almost No Leaves

Amaltas belongs to the bean family (Fabaceae), and it flowers the way no ordinary tree does. Botanical descriptions of the species note that flowering is profuse, with trees covered in yellow blossoms many times with almost no visible leaves — the tree sheds its foliage and blooms directly on bare wood, which is why the flower show looks so unreal. Each cluster is a hanging chain 8 to 18 inches long, and in Pakistan's plains the display arrives in the hot, dry stretch of late spring to early summer — roughly April into June, peaking around May when the first serious heat lands. The flowers attract bees, butterflies and especially carpenter bees (Xylocopa), the large solitary pollinators built to work the flower's structure.

The tree itself is a deciduous, fast-growing species reaching 10–20 metres (UF/IFAS sizes it at 30–40 feet in both height and spread) with a rounded crown and compound leaves. It is respected beyond Pakistan too: Thailand made it its national tree and national flower, and Kerala's state flower is the amaltas konna. Its wood is strong and famously durable. But understand the trade before you plant: this is a seasonal performer. For most of the year it is a plain, airy green tree with light shade — the golden weeks are its whole theatrical career. Plant it for those weeks, not as an evergreen canopy.

2. What Amaltas Asks For: Sun, Drainage, Patience

The University of Florida's extension factsheet (Gilman, 2019) is blunt about the deal: golden shower should be grown in full sun on well-drained soil. That is the whole religion. It tolerates clay, sand, loam, and both alkaline and acidic soils — which is why it handles Punjab's alkaline earth so well — and it is moderately drought-tolerant and moderately salt-tolerant once established. What it will not forgive is a spot where water stands: amaltas roots need drainage the way neem roots do, and a seasonally waterlogged pit will rot them quietly. The same field test we recommend for neem works here — dig the pit a few days early, and if it holds water after rain or irrigation, raise a mound or choose another spot.

Good news for streets and driveways: extension notes list amaltas among trees that are not a root problem near sidewalks, so it can line a compound wall or a lane without cracking everything in reach — the crown, not the roots, is what needs room. Plan for a mature spread of 9–12 metres: keep young trees 10–15 feet from buildings and boundary walls (our standard rule for school and home plantings), and don't squeeze it between structures where its eventual umbrella will fight the roofline. Sun is non-negotiable — a half-shaded amaltas grows into a leafy disappointment that blooms sparsely.

3. Planting Right: The Monsoon Window and First-Year Method

In Punjab we plant amaltas at the same window as everything else: at the monsoon onset, July–August, when deep soil moisture and easing temperatures give a new sapling its best start. Dig a pit about 2×2×2 feet, mix the excavated earth with well-rotted farmyard manure, and set the sapling so the nursery soil line sits level with the ground — buried stems rot, exposed roots panic. Water deeply immediately, then follow the deep-and-rare schedule any new tree needs: frequent light watering for the first couple of weeks, then deep weekly soakings through the growing season, with a mulch ring kept a hand's width from the trunk. Our first-year care guide has the full sourced method.

Amaltas is usually grown from seed — the long pods carry seeds embedded in a dark pulp, and they germinate readily when sown fresh. Two honest notes on the seed route: first, a seedling amaltas takes several years to its first bloom, so don't stand waiting in year one — the tree is building roots and a trunk while you wait, and grafted or nursery-grown flowering-size plants cost more precisely because they skip you some of that wait. Second, weather matters to the flower show itself: hot, dry weather as flowering approaches gives the heaviest bloom, while a damp, cloudy spell can mute it. You cannot control either — you can only give the tree sun and drainage and let it do its calendar.

4. The Poisonous-Pods Warning Every School Must Read

Here is the paragraph that matters most around children. After the flowers fade, amaltas hangs its signature crop: cylindrical pods 30–60 cm long (a foot to two feet), dark brown, rattling with seeds in sticky pulp. Those pods persist on the tree and on the ground through winter, and two facts collide around them. First, extension literature notes plainly that the tree is propagated by seed — and that the seeds are poisonous. Second, the pod pulp has a long history in traditional medicine as a purgative — the tree's very name in old texts, "purging cassia", says what it does — and medical sources are unanimous that self-medication with it should be strongly avoided.

Schools, homes with kids, pets: read this twice

Amaltas is a wonderful school-drive tree — the bloom wins every assembly photograph — but a tree that drops attractive, rattling, poisonous pods into a children's play area needs management. Plant it away from the sand-pit and the tuck shop, not at the gate where pods collect. After the pod season, rake and dispose of fallen pods. Teach the Green Scouts line we use: "amaltas ka phool dekho, daana nahi khao" — look at the flower, never eat the seed. If a child or pet swallows seeds, treat it as a poisoning case and get medical help immediately, bringing the pod with you so it can be identified.

5. What Amaltas Is Not

  • Not an evergreen shade canopy. It is deciduous and seasonal: bare or sparse through parts of the year, golden for weeks, plain green otherwise. For 12-month shade, pair it with an evergreen — neem or sukh chain — rather than replacing one with the other.
  • Not for wet feet. Standing water or a seasonally soggy pit will kill it slowly. Drainage is the one site factor you must fix before planting.
  • Not mess-free. Long pods persist through winter and drop; leaves drop seasonally too. Over a terrace, a fishbone chili bed or a parked car is the wrong place — over a lawn edge or a lane is right.
  • Not a home medicine cabinet. The pod pulp is a traditional purgative and the seeds are poisonous. This article is about planting trees, not treating people — for anything health-related, ask a qualified professional.
  • Not instant blooms. A young seedling spends years on roots and trunk before its first cascade. Everyone who loves amaltas waited too.

6. Care Through the Years: Mostly Light, Never Zero

Once past its establishment years, amaltas is a low-fuss citizen: moderate drought tolerance carries it through ordinary Punjab summers, it shrugs off light, brief frost (though persistent cold can damage it — young trees in frost-prone districts still deserve the jute-wrap treatment for their first two winters, same as neem), and its recorded disease issues — occasional leaf spot or mildew — are usually cosmetic rather than fatal. What it does need, every year, is the boring trinity: deep watering through the dry spell, the mulch ring renewed before summer, and a watchful eye in June when wind storms test a young canopy. Give it that and it hardens into one of the most self-sufficient flowering trees in Punjab.

The honesty part: buy the sapling, then buy the care

Amaltas's easy reputation is real — but it is earned after establishment, exactly like every other species we plant. A sapling's first two summers are as demanding as any young tree's, and our own care ledger for 500 mixed trees, at Rs.802 per tree per year, applies to amaltas like everything else.
