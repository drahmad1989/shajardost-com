The First Cold Spell Is Coming: Winter Care for Young Trees

20 October 2026 By Ahmad Baloch, Founder ShajarDost 7 min read

Punjab's planting season is closing. The monsoon saplings have hardened off, the evenings carry the first chill, and within weeks the fog will sit over the fields of Kasur and Pattoki for days at a time. This is the moment most plantation drives end and most trees are forgotten — and it is exactly backwards. Winter is when young trees die quietly: no dramatic wilt, no yellowing alarm — just a sapling that goes into December dry, unwrapped and mulch-less, and doesn't wake up properly in March. Our first-year care guide covered the survival math (79% of trees alive with proper aftercare versus 47% without it in a UK government trial); this guide is the winter chapter of that same care, written for our plains climate and sourced line by line.

1. Winter Kills in Three Quiet Ways

Punjab's winter looks gentle — bright afternoons, soup weather at worst. But three forces work on a young tree at once, and only one of them is actually "cold". First: dryness. Our winter is a dry season — weeks can pass without useful rain, and a young tree's shallow, still-growing roots sit in soil that is slowly losing its moisture. The Arbor Day Foundation warns that young, newly planted trees are much more susceptible to drought injury during winter than established ones — roots keep needing water even when the top of the tree looks asleep. Second: frost nights. On clear, windless nights the ground loses its day-heat fast, and a thin frost can form on lawns and fields — harmless to a five-year-old kikar, lethal to a one-year-old mango whose water-filled young tissues rupture when they freeze. Third, the one nobody suspects: rot. Cold soil that is kept constantly wet drains and dries slowly, and roots that would shrug off "too much water" in June can suffocate in it in January. Winter care is simply managing these three — water without waterlogging, warmth without suffocation, moisture storage without moisture stress.

2. Watering: The Midday Rule for Dry Spells

The most common winter mistake is the swing between two extremes — watering exactly like October, or abandoning the tree until March. The right answer sits in the middle, and the extension services that deal with real winters are surprisingly specific. The Arbor Day Foundation's guidance for young trees in dry winters is to keep watering every couple of weeks when there is no snow cover; translate that to Punjab, where snow is never the issue but long dry spells absolutely are: check the soil weekly, and give a deep soak whenever the top few inches have dried out. The Colorado State Forest Service adds the detail that matters most for frost country: winter watering is best done on warmer days — when the air is above roughly 5°C — and over a wide area around the root zone. For us that means one simple habit: water at midday on a bright winter day, never in the evening. Water applied at dusk in frost season sits cold on the roots all night; water applied at noon soaks in and the tree drinks before evening. Deep and occasional beats frequent and shallow in winter just as it does in summer — and if a rare winter rain arrives, skip the watering and let the sky do the work.

3. The Bori Wrap: Punjab's Oldest Frost Technology

Long before horticultural fleece existed, Punjab's nurserymen wrapped their young plants in bori — old jute sacking — every winter, and the modern science says they were right. The RHS's frost-protection guidance describes exactly this practice for tender plants: stems and branches wrapped in hessian (burlap) or fleece, which for heavier grades adds around 2°C of protection against frost. Two degrees sounds small until you meet the mango numbers: the University of Florida's IFAS extension notes that young mango trees begin to fail at just 29–30°F (about -2 to -1°C), while flowers and fruit are damaged below 40°F (about 4°C) — a cold spell that an adult tree shrugs off can end a sapling's life in one night. A loose jute wrap does not heat the tree; it slows the overnight heat loss and blocks the radiation frost that forms on bare bark, which is precisely why our own nurseries in Kasur tie bori on before the first fog spells.

Which trees actually need it? Young mango above all — wrap every winter for the first two to three years. Our neem guide already carries the hard lesson: young neem needs the bori for its first two winters — we learned that from real nursery losses in Kasur, and the tree's ideal-range biology (it dislikes cold far more than drought) explains why. Young amaltas, young moringa and any potted or gift sapling still in its first year all get the wrap too. Established hardy natives — kikar, safeda, sukh chain (whose mature trees tolerate light frost, per the Winrock Nitrogen Fixing Tree factsheet we cited in its species guide) — generally need nothing but mulch. The wrapping method matters: loose, from the base upward, jute not plastic — jute breathes and lets condensation escape, while tightly-tied plastic traps moisture against the bark and cooks the stem on sunny afternoons. On bright days, unwrap or loosen the top so the tree gets sun and air; tie it back by evening.

4. Mulch: The Blanket You Put On Before December

Winter mulch is not about warmth alone — it is about keeping the ground stable. The University of Minnesota Extension instructs exactly this for cold climates: cover the roots of newly planted trees with 3 to 4 inches (7–10 cm) of shredded wood mulch, because mulch and snow cover keep soil temperatures higher and steadier than bare ground. The Arbor Day Foundation explains what the mulch prevents: exposed planting sites crack open as the ground repeatedly freezes and thaws, and those cracks let cold, dry air reach and dry out the roots — mulch acts as the blanket that stops it. In Punjab the same ring does one more job: it holds the moisture of each rare watering or rain instead of letting the dry winter wind strip it. The rule from our care guide still applies with full force — donut, not volcano: start the ring a hand-width away from the trunk and spread outward 5–8 cm thick; mulch piled against the bark in cold, slow-drying months rots it faster than in any other season. Top the ring up in early December with dry leaves, katai hui ghaas or wood chips — the same free mulch that doubled summer growth.

The frost-night checklist (evening before a cold night)

  • Midday: deep soak the young and sensitive ones while the sun is up — moist soil holds more warmth than bone-dry soil going into the night.
  • Before sunset: bori wrap tied loose on mango, neem and year-one saplings; jute can stay on, plastic sheeting comes off.
  • Sunny morning after: unwrap or open the tops so stems get air and light; retie by late afternoon if another cold night is coming.
  • This week, not that night: top the mulch ring to 7–10 cm, kept off the trunk.

5. What Winter Is Not the Time For

Three temptations are best resisted until spring. Heavy pruning: a fresh cut just before the coldest weeks is an open wound on a tree that heals slowest in the cold — structural pruning waits for late winter or early spring, when cuts close fast and the tree's energy is rising. Late feeding: fertilizer pushed into a tree heading into the cold can drive a flush of soft new growth that the first frost then burns — in our ledger, winter is a watering-and-protection season, not a feeding one. Major transplanting: moving a young tree in the middle of the cold, dry months stacks three stresses at once; if a tree must move, plan it for early spring. Winter's real job list is short and humble — water on warm middays, wrap at dusk, mulch before December, and walk the rows watching for the quiet problems: a loosened tie, a goat-browsed stem, a mulch ring scattered by wind.

The honesty part: spring is counting season

Even with every line of this guide followed, some winter losses are normal — our budget assumes a 70–80% survival rate for young plantations, and our ledger carries a standing Rs.10,000 a year replacement line per 500 trees. The full-year cost of doing all of this properly — water, watchman, mulch, wraps and replacements — is what our cost transparency article put at Rs.802 per tree per year. What is not normal is losing trees to something this guide prevents: a dry December root ball, an unwrapped mango, mulch against a rotting trunk. Do the cheap things now, and let your March surprises be good ones.

"Sardi wo mausam nahi jab ped shor macha kar marte hain — ye wo mausam hai jab sab ko lagta hai kuch ho hi nahi raha. Asal kaam chhupa hota hai." — Winter is not when trees die loudly; it is when everything looks still and the real work is invisible.
