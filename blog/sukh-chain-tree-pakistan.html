Sukh Chain: The Tree That Laughs at Salt, Floods and Bad Soil

20 October 2026 By Ahmad Baloch, Founder ShajarDost 7 min read

Every Punjab planting list has a star — neem for the courtyard, amaltas for the bloom, shisham for the timber. And then there is the quiet fixer nobody photographs: sukh chain (سکھ چین), Pongamia pinnata — also written Millettia pinnata and once known as Derris indica. Its Urdu name translates to "peace and ease", and the tree lives up to it: a legume that pulls nitrogen out of the air, stands in waterlogged ground, shrugs off salt that would kill a neem, and produces seeds packed with 30–40% oil. If your land has a problem corner — the salty patch, the canal-bank strip, the low spot where every sapling drowned — this guide is for that corner. It also covers what sukh chain is not, because this tree has opinions about where it will live too.

1. Meet Pongamia: The Legume With a Lifesaver's Resume

Sukh chain is a medium-sized tree of the bean family — typically around 8 metres, with old trees reported up to 18 — carrying a short trunk and a dense, hemispherical crown of dark-green pinnate leaves. In season it hangs racemes of pink, light-purple or white flowers, followed by thick-walled elliptical pods (3–6 cm) that each hold a single seed. The taproot is thick and deep, with numerous well-developed laterals — which is one reason this tree can stand in places other species cannot. In Pakistan it is planted along canals, roads and compounds, and its botany explains the name botanists gave it: it is a true nitrogen-fixing tree, a legume that enriches the soil it stands in rather than merely surviving on it.

The most useful fact about the species, from Winrock International's nitrogen-fixing-tree fact sheet, is that it is one of the few NFTs whose seeds contain 30–40% oil — an oil that lit village lamps for generations. A 2022 peer-reviewed review of the species (Degani et al., Journal of Environmental Management) adds the modern framing: Pongamia is tolerant of drought, salinity and heavy metals in soil, and its combination of nitrogen fixing and tough siting gives it genuine climate-change-mitigation potential. That is a rare résumé: a tree that both feeds degraded soil and tolerates the abuse that created the degradation.

2. The Superpower: Salt, Waterlogging and Problem Soils

This is the section that makes sukh chain worth a place in every Punjab planter's vocabulary. The Winrock fact sheet's ecology notes read like a survival manual: the species thrives where annual rainfall runs 500–2500 mm, tolerates maximum temperatures of 27–38°C and minima of 1–16°C, and — critically — mature trees can withstand waterlogging and slight frost. It grows on most soil types from stony to sandy to clayey, including Vertisols (the heavy black cotton soils), and it is highly tolerant of salinity — a trait almost no mainstream Punjab shade tree shares. In its native range it stands along waterways and seashores with its roots literally in fresh or salt water.

Translate that to district Kasur or anywhere in the Punjab plains: the spots where neem refuses, where amaltas rots, where a citrus orchard would sulk — the canal-seepage flat, the saline patch with white crust in the dry season, the low corner that turns to a pond every monsoon — are precisely sukh chain's home ground. It is the species we reach for when the site itself is the enemy. Highest growth rates still come on well-drained soils with assured moisture, so "tough tree" does not mean "plant it in a brick": give it the same pit preparation you would give anything else, and it will reward you.

3. Where Sukh Chain Honestly Fails

No tree is invincible, and the fact sheet is specific about the failures. Sukh chain does not do well on dry sands — a loose, droughty sand profile defeats even this species, so the ridge of a sandier field is the wrong site. Best performance still requires well-drained soil with assured moisture; "withstands waterlogging when mature" is a mature-tree superpower, not a nursery promise. A young sapling — like every young tree — needs a real first-year watering schedule; modern research on young Pongamia orchards is literally built around irrigation scheduling for the establishment years (UF/IFAS, 2023), which tells you how seriously the young tree takes its water.

And the frost note has an age limit: mature trees take slight frost, but a sapling in its first two winters deserves the same jute-wrap (bori) and mulch-ring treatment we prescribe for neem in frost-prone districts of central and northern Punjab. If your spot is salty or wet and you were about to give up on trees altogether — don't. Sukh chain exists for that exact despair. But if your spot is bone-dry sand, pick a different species from our best trees for Punjab list.

4. The Oil: Real, Valuable — and Not for Your Kitchen

The seed oil deserves its own honest paragraph, because viral posts get it wrong in both directions. On the real side: the thick yellow-orange to brown oil is genuinely useful — mechanical expellers extract about 25% by volume, village crushers around 20% — and it has a documented history as lamp and cooking fuel in rural India, a lubricant, a water-paint binder, a pesticide base, and an input for soap-making and tanning. On the wrong side: the oil is not edible. Winrock's description is direct — it is bitter-tasting with a disagreeable aroma. Do not press your tree's seeds for salad oil; press them for a lamp, or better, leave them for planting stock.

Folk medicine is not our department

Traditional systems use pongamia oil for skin complaints and rheumatism, and the literature records these folk uses. We record them here as documentation, not advice. ShajarDost plants and cares for trees — we are not medical practitioners, and we do not recommend home treatment with any seed or oil. For anything health-related, ask a qualified professional.

5. Planting Right: The Familiar Monsoon Method

Good news: sukh chain follows the same planting religion as everything else we grow. Plant at the monsoon onset, July–August, into a pit about 2×2×2 feet amended with well-rotted farmyard manure, with the nursery soil line level with the ground, a deep watering immediately after, then the deep-and-rare schedule through the establishment years and a mulch ring kept clear of the trunk. Our first-year care guide carries the full sourced method. Propagation is easy — the fact sheet calls natural reproduction profuse by seed and common by root suckers, so fresh seed sown in season germinates readily, and a friend with a fruiting sukh chain can stock your whole boundary.

Give the tree the crown room its future demands: a mature sukh chain throws a dense hemispherical crown of roughly 8–15 metres across, so keep it out of tight courtyards and away from the roofline, and treat it as a boundary, canal-bank or park tree rather than a doorstep ornamental. In exposed sites, a loose stake for the first year keeps the young stem straight against monsoon winds — the same staking rule we use across our plantations.

6. Manage the Volunteers: The Honest Fine Print

Here is the paragraph most sukh chain praise skips. A tree that reproduces profusely by seed and commonly by root suckers does not stay politely where you planted it. Pods drop, float along water channels, and germinate on the banks; suckers push up a metre or two from the trunk. In regions where Pongamia was introduced far from its native range, this behaviour has earned it regulatory attention — Florida lists it as a prohibited high-invasion-risk species (UF/IFAS). Pakistan sits inside the species' natural neighbourhood, so the risk profile here is different, but the management lesson travels: a good steward handles the volunteers.

The practice is simple. After pod season, collect and dispose of pods you don't want seeding (or give them away to planters — a seedling gift beats a weed); pull volunteer seedlings while they are young, especially within a few metres of natural waterways where you don't own the banks; and cut suckers off at ground level each time they appear in the first years — they weaken with repetition. A sukh chain that is managed this way is a neighbourhood asset; one that isn't will plant itself all over the neighbour's Tube-well chamber and make you famous for the wrong reason.

7. What Sukh Chain Is Not

  • Not for dry sand. It is the wrong tree for loose, droughty sand profiles; choose from the Punjab best-trees list instead.
  • Not an edible-oil tree. The seed oil is bitter, ill-smelling and non-edible — it belongs in lamps, soap and pesticide chemistry, not your kitchen.
  • Not a timber investment. The wood burns well (4600 kcal/kg) but is not durable timber: it splits when sawn, invites insects, and is rated below quality species like shisham.
  • Not a zero-care sapling. The waterlogging and frost superpowers arrive with maturity; the first two years still need the full watering, mulch and frost-wrap routine.
  • Not a fence-neighbour. Profuse seedlings and root suckers will volunteer — manage them or they will manage you.

The first two summers and winters of a sukh chain sapling are exactly as demanding as any other young tree's, and our care ledger for 500 mixed trees, at Rs.802 per tree per year, applies here like everywhere else.
