Lahore's Air Is Breaking Records — Can Trees Help?

19 October 2026 By Ahmad Baloch, Founder ShajarDost 8 min read

On 2 November 2024, Lahore's Air Quality Index touched 1,067 — the first time any reading in the city had crossed the 1,000 mark. A senior Punjab environment official told reporters, "We have never reached a level of 1,000." Primary schools shut across the province; institutions stayed closed into the third week of November. Parents kept children indoors, hospitals set up smog counters, and the city breathed air that measured dozens of times above the World Health Organization's guideline. We plant trees for a living, and people ask us the same question every smog season: is this what trees are for? The honest answer has two halves — and you deserve both.

What actually happened to Lahore's air

Lahore's PM2.5 — the fine particles small enough to pass from lungs into blood — averaged roughly 74 µg/m³ in 2024, about fifteen times the WHO annual guideline of 5 µg/m³, making it among the world's most polluted major cities in IQAir's annual ranking. The following year's report showed improvement — Lahore placed eighth globally rather than first — but eighth on that list is not comfort; it is still deep in the unhealthy band, and every winter the same ingredients return: temperature inversions that trap the air like a lid, crop-residue burning upwind, traffic and industry within it, and construction dust on top. The November 2024 episode was simply the lid clamped shut over the busiest burning season.

November 2024 mein poora Punjab band hua — schools, offices, sab. Hawa ne record toota, magar asal record wo nahi tha. Asal record wo hai jo har moohallay ki clinic mein likha jata hai: saans ki bimariyan.

What trees genuinely do — with the numbers

Trees are not a gimmick; their measurable benefits in cities are real and well-studied. A modelling study of 93 European cities published in The Lancet found that if urban tree cover were raised to 30%, cities would cool by an average of 0.4°C — with local hotspots up to 5.9°C cooler — and about 39.5% of deaths attributable to urban heat islands could be prevented. A separate London study estimated the city's existing urban forest avoided around 153 heat-attributable deaths from 2015 to 2022, and found that the leafiest tenth of the city ran about 2°C cooler at night than the barest tenth during heatwaves. For Pakistan's 45°C summers, that is not decoration; that is public health infrastructure with leaves on it.

Heat is where trees are strongest. Their second job is filtration: leaf surfaces capture dust and particulates, hedges and tree belts along roads measurably reduce the dust that drifts onto footpaths and into homes, and a healthy canopy buffers noise. Their third job is the one nobody prices until it is gone — shade over parked cars, playgrounds, and the courtyard where your grandmother sits, which is why our Punjab species guide starts with broad, shady natives rather than ornamentals.

What trees cannot do (the honesty box)

A tree cannot filter a traffic jam, and it cannot stop a temperature inversion. Winter smog in Lahore is produced by combustion — vehicles, industry, brick kilns, crop burning — and those emissions must be cut at source; foliage sits above and beside the problem, not inside the exhaust pipe. A city that plants trees while its sources burn unchecked is using plantation as a press release. That is greenwashing, and it is the one thing we refuse to do. Plant trees and demand source control — the two are a team, not substitutes.

Where trees help most in a city like Lahore

  • Dense native clusters in parks and green belts — the Miyawaki approach builds a layered mini-forest that cools and filters a small area within 2–3 years, instead of a row of sticks for a decade.
  • Tree and hedge belts along roads and schools — the closest foliage to exhaust height and dust sources catches the most; this is where Green Scouts school plantings earn their keep.
  • Courtyards and rooftops in dense neighbourhoods — one sohanjna or amaltas shading a west-facing wall changes that room's afternoon entirely.
  • Water bodies' edges — shade slows evaporation and keeps the cooling effect of water working longer into summer.

We operate around Pattoki in Kasur district, roughly an hour from Lahore, and what we see on our own sites matches the literature: a young cluster that is watered and guarded for three years turns into a patch where afternoon ground temperature and dust both drop visibly. The city-scale numbers come from canopy percentages — meaning every courtyard, school and green belt is a pixel of the answer.

So, can trees fix Lahore's air?

Fix? No. Help, measurably, where it matters most — heat, dust, shade, and the mental survival of everyone who lives through June and November? The evidence says yes, strongly. Trees are one wing of a two-winged answer; cutting emissions at source is the other, and neither flies alone. What you can do this season: plant the right species in the right place near your home, commit to its three years of care (our real-cost article tells you exactly what that takes), and keep demanding that the sources — vehicles, kilns, industry, stubble — be cleaned up. A city that does both is a city whose children breathe.
