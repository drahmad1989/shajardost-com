7 Tree Plantation Myths in Pakistan — Busted

19 October 2026 By Ahmad Baloch, Founder ShajarDost 8 min read

Every planting season, the same sentences travel across Punjab — in speeches, on banners, in WhatsApp forwards. "Rs.100 mein ped lag jata hai." "Ped laga diya, kaam mukammal." "Eucalyptus lagao, sab se tez barhta hai." Most of these sentences sound wise. Some are half-true. A few are quietly killing millions of saplings every year. We plant and care for trees around Pattoki in Kasur district, and we keep actual records of what survives and what it costs. Here are the seven myths we fight most often — and what the data actually says.

Myth 1: "Ped laga diya — kaam khatam"

This is the mother of all myths, because every other myth grows from it. The photo is taken, the inauguration is done, and everyone walks away. But planting is day one of a roughly three-year promise: after that period a native tree can usually stand on its own, and before it, it is a snack for goats and a victim of the next dry month. The best government-funded field trial we know of — the UK's Trees Outside Woodlands programme, spread over 16 plots — measured 79% survival where aftercare was provided versus 47% where it was not, over 3.5 years. Nearly half the uncared saplings died. On our own sites we budget the same way: care, not planting, is the real work.

Myth 2: "Rs.100 mein ped lag jata hai"

Rs.100 buys a sapling — maybe. We published our full internal ledger for 500 trees in our cost article: about Rs.150 one-time to plant (sapling, pit, soil prep) and about Rs.802 per tree per year in care — water, a watchman, electricity, fertilizer. Three years of establishment comes to roughly Rs.2,556 per tree. The sapling is about 6% of the real bill. When a campaign quotes only the sapling price, it is counting the photograph, not the tree.

Myth 3: "Eucalyptus lagao — sab se tez barhta hai"

Fast? Yes, remarkably. A good default choice for community planting? No. Eucalyptus is a heavy water user, its leaf litter and root chemistry suppress undergrowth, and almost nothing in a Pakistani food web depends on it — few birds feed on it, almost no native insect life thrives on it. On water-stressed sites it competes directly with crops and groundwater. It has legitimate industrial uses (fast biomass, timber on wet or marginal land), but as the automatic answer to "which tree should we plant in Punjab?" it fails — which is why our Punjab species guide leads with natives like kikar, shisham, sohanjna and amaltas, drawn from the Punjab Forest Department's own lists.

Myth 4: "Bohat se ped lagane se jungle ban jata hai"

Plant one species in neat rows and you get a plantation — a crop — not a forest. A forest is a layered structure: canopy trees, mid-level trees, shrubs, ground cover, each sheltering the next. Rows of a single species dry out the soil, invite one pest to a full meal, and give wildlife nothing to live on. This is exactly the problem the Miyawaki method was built to solve: 3–4 native saplings per square metre across four layers, which grow denser and survive drought better because the layers shade and feed each other. Quantity is easy. Structure is what makes a forest.

Myth 5: "Ghar ke paas ped lagane se deewar toot jati hai"

Half-true, and the half matters. Big aggressive roots near foundations and sewer lines do cause damage — which is a rule about species and placement, not a ban on trees. Sohanjna and amaltas are polite near homes; kikar, neem and shisham need distance from walls. Roots follow water, so a leaking pipe attracts them more than the species itself. The correct sentence is: right tree, right place, right distance — not "no trees near the house". Millions of courtyards across Punjab are 4–6°C cooler in June because someone ignored this myth a generation ago.

Myth 6: "Ped lagana sarkar ya NGO ka kaam hai"

Governments plant in millions and lose in millions, because mass campaigns are the hardest place to provide aftercare. The UK trial mentioned above worked because community groups and landowners ran the plots with support — small, local, accountable. That is the model we follow: schools through our Green Scouts programme, courtyards through the Free Plants wall, donors who get updates as their trees establish. A hundred cared-for trees beat ten thousand abandoned ones on every measure that matters — survival, shade, habitat and honesty.

Myth 7: "Ped climate change ka poora hal hain"

Here is the myth that sounds greenest, and it is the one we refuse to say. The evidence for trees in cities is genuinely strong: a Lancet-published modelling study of 93 European cities found that raising tree cover to 30% could have prevented about 39.5% of heat-attributable deaths, with average cooling of 0.4°C and local cooling up to 5.9°C. London's urban forest avoided an estimated 153 heat deaths between 2015 and 2022. But trees do not filter winter smog at exhaust-pipe height, and they do not absorb a traffic jam. Planting cannot be the excuse that lets pollution sources off the hook — in our Lahore air article we say plainly what trees can fix and what they cannot. Honest tree-planters make better environmentalists than enthusiastic ones.

Sab se bada jhoot numbers ka nahi, tasveer ka hai: naya ped, nayi shalwar, camera ka flash — aur teen saal baad ek sookhi lakri. Jo ped zinda rehta hai wo announcement nahi, intezam hota hai.

The one-line summaries

  • Myth 1: Planting is day one — care for three summers or expect half your trees to die.
  • Myth 2: A tree is Rs.2,556 over three years, not Rs.100 once.
  • Myth 3: Eucalyptus is fast, not right — natives first on water-stressed land.
  • Myth 4: Rows are a crop; layers are a forest.
  • Myth 5: Right species, right place — trees and houses have always been friends.
  • Myth 6: Local, small and cared-for beats mass and abandoned.
  • Myth 7: Trees are a powerful tool, not a total solution — say both.

Sources & data note

• Heat-mortality figures: Iungman et al., "Cooling cities through urban green infrastructure" modelling of 93 European cities, The Lancet (2023), via ISGlobal/EurekAlert release; London canopy study: Taylor et al., Environmental Research Letters (2024) — 153 avoided heat deaths 2015–2022.

• Cost figures: ShajarDost internal care ledger, 500 trees, Pattoki / Kasur district — published in full in our cost article.

• Species guidance: Punjab Forest Department species lists (kikar, shisham, phulai, jand, sohanjna, amaltas and others).
